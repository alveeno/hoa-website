E.J. Carr Late Disgorged

The bottle at the centre of the story — a Tasmanian sparkling wine, decades in the making, now judged perfect.

Why “bread” and “toast” in a wine?

When a sparkling wine rests for years on its spent yeast (a process called autolysis), it develops savoury, bakery-like aromas — brioche, toast, warm bread, roasted nuts. These flavours are a hallmark of the very finest, long-aged bubbles, and they are exactly why this wine reads as so profound.

The secret to the style

“Late Disgorged,” explained simply

In sparkling winemaking, the bubbles are created by a second fermentation inside the bottle. That process leaves behind a deposit of spent yeast, called the lees. Before the wine can be sold, that deposit has to be removed — a step called disgorgement.

Most sparkling wines are disgorged after two or three years. A “Late Disgorged” wine is deliberately left to rest on its lees for far, far longer — many years — before it is opened up and finished. This long, patient ageing is what builds the deep, toasty, savoury complexity that critics prize.

The takeaway

“Late Disgorged” is a signal of extra time, patience and complexity. The 1998 was the very first wine House of Arras ever released under this prestige label.

Rarity

Why a magnum, and why so few exist

A magnum is special

A magnum holds 1.5 litres — twice a normal bottle. Wine matures more slowly and gracefully in this larger format, which is why magnums are so prized by collectors for long ageing.

A “winemaker’s release”

This magnum was held back deep in the winery’s own museum cellar, resting on its lees for decades before being disgorged — a personal reserve from the team that made it.

Only a handful remain

Just a rare few of these magnums are left in the cellar at House of Arras. The wine that earned a perfect score is, by its nature, almost impossible to find.
