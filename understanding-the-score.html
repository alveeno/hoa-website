The 100-Point Score

What a perfect 100 really means

No wine knowledge required. Here is how the world’s most powerful wine scale works — and why a perfect score is the rarest praise in the business.

First, the publication

What is the Robert Parker Wine Advocate?

The Wine Advocate is, simply put, the most influential wine-rating publication in the world. It was founded in 1978 by the American critic Robert Parker, who became so trusted that his scores could make or break a wine.

Think of it as the wine world’s equivalent of a definitive, industry-standard review — the rating that collectors, restaurants, retailers and winemakers around the globe pay attention to. When the Wine Advocate gives a wine a very high score, demand surges, prices climb, and bottles can sell out almost overnight. Its 100-point system became the template the entire industry now uses.

Why its opinion carries weight

A score from the Wine Advocate isn’t just one person’s taste — it is a benchmark the global wine trade treats as authoritative. That is exactly why a perfect score makes headlines.

The scale

How the 100-point system works

Here is the surprising part: wines aren’t scored from 0. Every wine starts at 50 points just for being wine, and earns its way up from there. In practice, the meaningful range runs from 50 to 100.

Extraordinary

A profound, benchmark wine. 100 is perfect — the top of the entire scale. This is where the House of Arras 1998 landed.

70–79

Drinkable but with little distinction.

60–69
Below average

Noticeable flaws.

50–59
Unacceptable

The floor of the scale.

Reaching 96–100 at all is rare. Reaching a flawless 100 is the ceiling — the score critics reserve for wines they consider as fine as the category can possibly be.

Inside the number

Where the 100 points come from

A perfect score means a wine earned full marks in every category the critic assesses. Under the Wine Advocate’s published method, the 100 points are built like this:

Starting base (every wine)50
Colour & appearance+5
Aroma & bouquet (the smell)+15
Flavour & finish (the taste)+20
Overall quality & ageing potential+10
Add it up

50 + 5 + 15 + 20 + 10 = 100. A perfect score means the critic found nothing to deduct — full marks for appearance, aroma, flavour, finish and overall greatness.

The record

Why this particular 100 made history

Plenty of still wines have earned 100 points over the years. But among sparkling wines, a perfect score had only ever gone to Champagne — the famous region in north-east France.

For 48 years — since the rating system began in 1978 — no sparkling wine from anywhere else on Earth had reached 100. The House of Arras 1998 is the first non-Champagne sparkling wine ever to do it, and the first from an Australian sparkling producer. A wine from cool, remote Tasmania is now rated the equal of the world’s greatest Champagnes.

What a perfect 100 feels like, in other worlds

Borrow a feeling from somewhere you already know. A 100-point wine is that level of achievement.

The perfect 10

A gymnast nailing a routine so flawless the judges have nothing left to deduct. That same idea — perfection, no faults — is what a 100-point wine represents.

Universal acclaim

A film that every major critic calls a masterpiece. A 100 is the wine equivalent of unanimous, top-of-the-class recognition.

Full marks

A final exam returned with every single question correct. Not “excellent” — literally nothing more to award.

The summit

Like a chef earning the highest possible honour in fine dining. It places the wine among the very best in the world, full stop.

The bottom line

How rare — and how powerful — is a 100?

The Wine Advocate tastes many thousands of wines every year. The vast majority never reach the “extraordinary” 96–100 tier at all, and a flawless 100 is rarer still. It is not a score handed out freely — it is the exception of exceptions.

And it carries real-world weight. A perfect score can turn a wine into an instant icon: prices rise, collectors compete, and bottles vanish from shelves. For a producer, a single 100 can define a decades-long reputation.

So, in one line

House of Arras didn’t just make a very good wine. It made one the most authoritative voice in wine judged perfect — the first of its kind in nearly half a century.
